01 the exchange rate

One wire, two speeds

An H100 is two territories that barely resemble each other. On one side, 132 streaming multiprocessors, each feeding tensor cores that retire small matrix multiplications as single instructions — 989 TFLOP/s of dense BF16 arithmetic in aggregate. On the other, 80 GB of HBM3: DRAM dies stacked into towers and bonded millimeters from the compute die, streaming 3.35 TB/s. Between them runs one wire, and nothing happens on this chip except by crossing it. The compute side holds no data worth mentioning; the memory side cannot add two numbers. An H100 is a fast machine and a slow machine sharing a package, and every cost in inference — every millisecond, every token, every dollar — is set by the exchange rate between them.

Divide the two speeds and mind the units. 989 TFLOP/s over 3.35 TB/s is 295 FLOPs per byte — the seconds cancel, leaving a pure exchange rate. In the time HBM takes to deliver one byte, the tensor cores could have retired 295 floating-point operations. Call it the ridge. It is a property of the silicon and nothing else. The matching property of a workload is its arithmetic intensity: the FLOPs it performs divided by the bytes it moves from HBM — also seconds-free, also a pure ratio. Every byte that crosses the wire arrives owing 295 FLOPs; a workload whose intensity cannot cover the debt leaves tensor cores dark, and no scheduler, kernel, or driver setting changes the arithmetic.

The roofline model turns the comparison into geometry. Plot intensity on the x-axis and achieved FLOP/s on the y, both log scale. Attainable performance is min(989 TFLOP/s, intensity × 3.35 TB/s): a slope that climbs with intensity, capped by a flat roof, meeting at the ridge. Left of 295 you are memory-bound — performance is your intensity times the bandwidth, full stop. Right of 295 you are compute-bound — the wire keeps up and arithmetic is the limit. The model assumes perfect overlap: time = max(bytes ÷ bandwidth, FLOPs ÷ peak), no latency, no launch overhead, no occupancy stalls. That makes it an upper bound, not a prediction; real kernels live below both lines. But the roofline has no tunable parameters, and the one thing it tells you — which resource you exhaust first — is the one thing it almost never gets wrong.

Now place decode. Generating one token at batch 1 performs two FLOPs per weight — one multiply, one add — about 13.5 GFLOPs for the 7B model. To perform them it must stream every fp16 weight plus the sequence's KV cache out of HBM: roughly 16 GB at a 4,096-token context. That is intensity 0.867, some 340× left of the ridge. The slope prices it instantly: 0.867 × 3.35 TB/s ≈ 2.9 TFLOP/s, which is 0.29% of peak, and 4.82 ms per token as a hard floor — the floor is literally 16 GB divided by 3.35 TB/s. Prefill is the same weights on the same silicon at intensity 502: right of the ridge, compute-bound, 512 prompt tokens in 7.25 ms. A 580× intensity gap separates two phases of the same request on the same chip — and that gap, not any property of the model, is the origin of nearly every serving technique that exists.

Neither coordinate of your dot is fixed. Batching attacks the denominator through reuse: the 14 GB of weights are read once and serve every sequence in the batch — but each sequence drags its own KV cache across the wire, so batch multiplies those bytes too and the scaling is sub-linear. Intensity climbs from 0.867 at batch 1 to only about 5.2 at batch 32, and 207 tokens/s becomes 1,296 — a 6.3× gain for 32× the batch; near-linearity holds only while the KV cache stays small next to the weights. And that batch-32 fp16 figure is hypothetical on this chip: weights plus 32 sequences of 4,096-token KV cache total 82.8 GB, which does not fit in 80 GB of HBM. Quantization shrinks the bytes themselves: int8 halves the stream and batch-1 decode doubles to 415 tokens/s; int4 halves it again to 830 — bandwidth-linear, exactly as the slope demands. Precision also moves the ridge: FP8 tensor cores run at 1979 TFLOP/s, which pushes the ridge to 1979 ÷ 3.35 ≈ 590 FLOPs per byte — a taller roof that is harder to stand under, since a workload now owes 590 FLOPs per byte before the extra compute means anything. Every lever in serving is one of three moves on this chart: reuse more FLOPs per byte, move fewer bytes, or raise the roof — and the third is worthless without the first two.

HBM is one rung on a ladder, and 295 is HBM's ridge specifically. Above it sit registers and each SM's shared-memory SRAM — on the order of a terabyte per second per SM, but only tens of megabytes chip-wide. Below it, NVLink moves roughly 900 GB/s aggregate between peer H100s, and below that PCIe drops down to host DRAM, each rung swapping bandwidth for capacity. Every rung has its own roofline, and a rung's roofline taxes only the traffic that touches it — so the deepest optimizations in this field are not arithmetic tricks but relocations. FlashAttention does not remove a single FLOP from attention; it tiles the computation so the score matrix is born and dies in SRAM, where the local ridge is an order of magnitude more forgiving, and HBM never sees the traffic. Tensor parallelism runs the other direction: it splits the weights across GPUs to buy capacity and aggregate bandwidth, and pays with an all-reduce of about 2 × d_model × batch activations (× bytes per element) per layer, per GPU pair, over the NVLink rung. Same min(), different rung.

Hold the chart; the rest of this piece is drawn on it. Decode is a dot crawling low on the slope. Prefill is a dot pinned under the roof. Continuous batching is a scheduler herding decode dots rightward. Quantization redraws the dot at half or a quarter of its byte cost; speculative decoding smuggles several tokens through one crossing; long context drags the dot left as KV bytes swell the denominator, until around 26,700 tokens of fp16 MHA cache reading the cache costs as much as reading the model. Serving optimization is not making the GPU faster; it is moving your dot.

the wrong picture

Tokens per second is a compute number: a 7B model is a lot of math per token, so decode speed tracks the GPU's TFLOPs, and a chip with twice the TFLOPs generates twice as fast.

what actually happens

Batch-1 decode extracts 0.29% of the H100's arithmetic — the 13.5 GFLOPs per token are trivial; streaming the 16 GB of weights and KV cache per token is not. Token latency is bytes divided by 3.35 TB/s, so more bandwidth helps immediately, while more TFLOPs does nearly nothing until batching or prefill pushes intensity toward the 295 ridge.

worth stealing

Whenever a fast unit is fed by a slow channel, divide their speeds before doing anything else: peak throughput over channel bandwidth gives a break-even reuse count — operations you must perform per byte (or per row, per request, per round trip) just to keep the unit busy. Workloads below that count are priced entirely by the channel, and investment in the unit changes nothing. The same arithmetic governs CPUs against DRAM, services against their databases, and disks against networks: find the ridge, determine which side of it you live on, and only then decide what to optimize.

02 the resident

A model is a memory object

Strip away the checkpoint format, the framework, the architecture diagram, and what remains is what the GPU actually sees: bytes at addresses. A Llama-2-7B-shaped model is 6.74 billion learned numbers — "7B" is a round-up — and at two bytes each in bf16 that is a 14 GB slab which must sit whole in HBM before the first token comes out. Not paged in on demand, not partially resident: loaded once at startup, pinned for the life of the server. The governing equation is the trivial one, bytes = params × precision, and everything a serving stack does — quantizing, sharding, batching, evicting — is downstream of that multiplication. A model's operational identity is its byte count, not its parameter count. The memory system never learns what it is holding.

The slab has a repeating internal anatomy. Each of the 32 layers holds seven big matrices. Four belong to attention — Wq, Wk, Wv, Wo, each 4096 × 4096: 16.8M parameters, 33.6 MB apiece at two bytes. This model runs full multi-head attention, 32 KV heads matching 32 query heads, so K and V projections get no width discount. Three belong to the SwiGLU MLP — gate and up projections of 4096 × 11008 and a down projection back: 45.1M parameters, 90.2 MB apiece. One layer totals 202M parameters, about 405 MB, and the three MLP matrices are two-thirds of it. By bytes, a transformer is mostly feed-forward network; attention is a minority shareholder. Stack 32 identical layers, add the two vocabulary matrices — a 32,000 × 4096 input embedding and a separate, untied 32,000 × 4096 lm_head, 131M parameters each — and the ledger closes at 6.74B.

Those two vocabulary matrices deserve a closer look, because they are the same shape and behave nothing alike. The input embedding is not a matmul at all — it is a table lookup. Each incoming token gathers exactly one 4096-wide row, 8 KB at bf16, and the other 31,999 rows stay cold. It is the only large matrix in the model that is never streamed in full. The lm_head is its mirror image: a genuine GEMM against all 262 MB, every single step, to score all 32,000 vocabulary entries and produce one row of logits per sequence. Same 131M parameters; one costs 8 KB per token, the other costs 262 MB per step. Shape does not determine cost — access pattern does. Untying the pair, as this model does, spends 524 MB of the slab where a tied model would spend half that.

Now the property that separates a dense model from almost every workload a memory hierarchy was designed for: there is no hot subset — the working set is the model. Producing one token multiplies the hidden state through all seven matrices of all 32 layers, then the lm_head. Every parameter participates in every forward pass, so no cache tier can help; everything is touched, every few milliseconds, forever. The consolation prize is predictability. The weights are read in a fixed order — layer 0 through layer 31, the same large contiguous tensors in the same sequence every step — which is the friendliest access pattern a DRAM system can be offered, and why the idealized time = bytes ÷ bandwidth model is nearly achievable in practice. The arithmetic: one layer's 405 MB streams in about 121 microseconds at 3.35 TB/s; the whole 14 GB slab in about 4.2 ms. That is a floor. A mixture-of-experts model breaks the no-hot-subset rule deliberately — a router sends each token through a few expert MLPs and reads a fraction of the parameters per token, buying capacity without buying bytes. A dense 7B gets no such discount.

The second structural fact: parameters are not FLOPs, and the same 14 GB read serves one token or thirty-two. A weight, once fetched, buys exactly one multiply and one add per token in the batch — two floating-point operations for a two-byte read, an arithmetic intensity of 0.867 at batch 1 against the H100's ridge of 295 FLOPs per byte. The read is fixed; the work it enables scales with the batch. At batch 1, decode costs 4.82 ms per step — 4.18 ms of that is the weight stream, 0.64 ms is the 2.15 GB of KV cache riding along — and 99.7 percent of the tensor cores' capacity goes unused. At batch 32 the identical weight fetch performs 32 tokens' worth of arithmetic in nearly the same time. This is why the parameter count tells you the per-step latency floor of a model but almost nothing about its throughput ceiling; the gap between those two numbers is the entire economics of the later chapters.

One more property hides in plain sight: the slab is immutable. Serving never writes a weight. That is what makes distribution clean — tensor parallelism partitions the matrices across GPUs exactly once, at load time, and there is no coherence protocol, no invalidation, no synchronization of the resident set, ever. The only traffic that crosses the interconnect is activations: roughly 2 × d_model × batch × bytes per layer per all-reduce over NVLink's ~900 GB/s, a rounding error next to the local HBM streams. Training has none of this luxury — its weights change every step and must be kept in agreement. Because inference weights are read-only, the only thing distributed serving ever has to move is activations, and the slab itself can be treated the way an OS treats a shared read-only text segment: map it, never touch it again.

Which brings the 80 GB box into focus from the other side. The model claims 14 GB and the remaining 66 GB is not slack — it is the product. Every concurrent sequence at 4,096 tokens of context brings 2.15 GB of KV cache, so the vacancy admits 30 residents and no more; batch 32 would want 82.8 GB and does not fit on the card. Capacity bounds concurrency; bandwidth bounds latency; both are denominated in the same bytes, which is why the precision toggle moves both at once — at int8 the rent drops to 7 GB and 67 sequences fit, at int4 to 3.5 GB and 142, business the quantization chapter will conduct in full. The empty part of HBM, not the full part, is what a serving stack actually sells. The resident is fixed cost; the vacancy is inventory.

the wrong picture

Seven billion parameters means about 7 GB, and once the model is "loaded" the GPU runs it the way a CPU runs a program — a hot inner loop living in cache while most of the weights sit idle, so bigger models are merely slower to start.

what actually happens

At two bytes per bf16 parameter it is 14 GB, and a dense model has no hot loop: every token's forward pass streams essentially all 14 GB back out of HBM, every step, in a fixed order — about 4.2 ms per sweep at 3.35 TB/s. The working set equals the model, so caches cannot help; only fewer bytes per parameter (quantization) or fewer parameters touched per token (MoE) shrinks the read.

worth stealing

When a workload's working set equals its data, locality optimization is finished — no cache tier can save you, and the mandatory full read becomes the unit of cost. Only two levers remain: shrink the bytes themselves, or amortize each mandatory read across more useful work per pass. And when a fixed resident set shares capacity with per-client state that grows, size the system around the leftover — the resident is fixed cost, and the vacancy is the resource you are actually scheduling and selling.

03 one token’s birth

From prompt to logits to token

A model never sees text. It sees integers from a fixed vocabulary of 32,000, and the mapping from text to integers was decided before training ever started. Llama-2's tokenizer is a SentencePiece BPE model: start from characters (with ▁ marking word-leading whitespace), repeatedly merge the most frequent adjacent pair in the training corpus, stop after roughly 32,000 merges — plus 256 byte-fallback tokens as the escape hatch for anything the learned pieces don't cover. The result is a vocabulary of subwords — common strings get one id, rare strings decompose into pieces, down to single bytes if nothing else matches, so no input is ever out-of-vocabulary. Why 32,000 subwords instead of 256 characters? Because sequence length is the currency of everything in this article. Every token position costs one full pass through 14 GB of weights and 524 KB of permanent KV residency; the tokenizer's job is to buy as much text as possible per position. A 32k BPE token carries three to four characters of typical English; byte-level encoding would multiply positions by that factor — proportionally more decode steps, proportionally more KV cache, and a quadratic blowup in attention on top.

Ids in hand, the first operation is not a matmul but a gather: id 7423 selects row 7,423 of the 32,000×4096 embedding matrix — an 8 KB read at fp16. That 4096-float vector is the residual stream, and it is the only thing that flows. Thirty-two layers each read it, compute an attention output and an MLP output, and add them back in; RMSNorm rescales the vector before each sublayer, and the additions accumulate. The layers do not replace the state — they refine it. The model's entire running belief about what comes next is 8 KB per position; the 14 GB of weights are static machinery the vector passes through.

After layer 32: one final RMSNorm, then the unembedding — the 4096-vector times a 4096×32,000 matrix. That is 131 million weights, 262 MB at fp16, and in this model deliberately not tied to the input embedding; the two matrices learned different jobs. The output is 32,000 raw scores, the logits, one per vocabulary entry. At the 2-FLOPs-per-parameter convention used throughout, this matmul costs 262 MFLOPs — under 2% of the ~14 GFLOPs the whole token took. One serving detail worth knowing: during prefill a 2048-token prompt produces 2,048 hidden states, but only the last one needs unembedding, so engines skip the other 2,047 logit rows entirely. Seven billion parameters of computation exit the network as one 32,000-number opinion.

Logits are not probabilities; softmax makes them probabilities — exponentiate each score, divide by the sum. Temperature is a single division applied first: every logit becomes z/T. Division by a positive constant preserves order, which gives temperature its one crucial and widely misunderstood property. Temperature never changes the model's ranking — it redistributes probability mass across a ranking that is already fixed. As T approaches 0 the mass collapses onto the argmax and sampling becomes greedy; at T = 1 you read the trained distribution as-is; above 1 the gaps between exponentiated scores shrink and the tail fattens. The network computed nothing differently at T = 0.2 versus T = 1.5. The same 32,000 numbers were read two different ways.

The sampler is a truncation rule plus one random draw. Greedy: take the maximum. Top-k: sort, keep the k highest, renormalize. Top-p (nucleus): sort, keep the smallest prefix whose cumulative probability reaches p — an adaptive k that widens when the model is uncertain. Min-p: keep every token whose probability is at least min_p times the top token's — a threshold that scales with the model's own confidence, tight when it is sure, permissive when it is not. All of it — the 262 MFLOP unembedding, a softmax over 32,000 floats, a sort — is microseconds against the 4.82 ms the memory-bound forward pass just spent. Sampling is the one stage of inference that never appears on the roofline, and the only stage where anything is decided.

Because the decision happens here, control lives here. Structured decoding — JSON mode, regex constraints, full grammars — is a mask on the logits: before softmax, every token that would violate the grammar is set to −∞, and the sampler cannot emit it. The forward pass is untouched; a CPU-side state machine tracks which tokens are currently legal, and the GPU never learns the grammar exists. (Speculative decoding's exactness guarantee — accept a draft token with probability min(1, p_target/p_draft), resample from the normalized excess otherwise — is likewise pure sampler-stage arithmetic.) Determinism lives here too, with a caveat that surprises people: temperature 0 selects the argmax deterministically, but the logits themselves are not bit-stable on a real server — batch composition changes kernel tiling and floating-point reduction order, and two logits separated by 10⁻⁶ can swap ranks between runs. Greedy decoding makes the sampler deterministic; it does not make the system deterministic. A seed pins the sampler's RNG. Nothing short of fixed numerics pins the logits.

The chosen id is not yet text. BPE tokens are byte sequences, and token boundaries do not respect character boundaries: a multi-byte UTF-8 character — an emoji, a CJK glyph — can be split across two consecutive tokens, so a streaming detokenizer buffers bytes until they form valid UTF-8 before emitting anything. SentencePiece adds its own trap: the leading space belongs to the token (▁the), so decoding tokens one at a time and concatenating mangles spacing — real detokenizers decode the growing sequence and emit the diff against what the client has already seen. This is why streaming APIs send text deltas, not tokens. Then the loop closes: the new id is appended to the sequence, its K and V vectors join the cache, and it becomes the entire input to the next forward pass — one 8 KB embedding row in, 4.82 ms of memory traffic, 32,000 logits out, again. A conversation is this loop, run a few hundred times.

the wrong picture

The network "chooses" the next word somewhere inside its 32 layers, and temperature makes it compute more adventurous choices — turning the dial changes what the model does during the forward pass.

what actually happens

The forward pass is a fixed function that ends in 32,000 logits and is identical at every temperature. Choice happens after the network, in microseconds of post-processing: temperature, top-p, min-p, grammar masks, and seeds all operate on that one logit vector and never touch a weight or a FLOP of the pass. "Creativity" is a property of how the distribution is read, not of what the model computed.

worth stealing

When a system has one expensive deterministic core and many cheap decisions, push the decisions to the boundary where the core's output is narrowest. Here a 14 GB forward pass funnels into a single 32,000-number vector, and that waist is where determinism, format guarantees, and policy get enforced — at negligible cost, without touching the hot path. The same shape recurs everywhere: query planners emit plans that policy hooks veto, routers compute tables that ACLs filter. Control belongs at the narrow waist, not inside the engine.

04 the industry’s state

The cache that runs the industry

Strip attention down to its data dependency. To emit token N+1, every one of the 32 layers needs the key and value vectors of all N tokens that came before — not a summary, not the recent few, all of them, at full width. The weights are stateless: the same 14 GB serves every request, every step, forever. Attention is the exception, and it forces a choice with exactly two branches: re-derive the past on every step, or write it down once. Attention turns generation into a stateful process, and the only design freedom is which currency the state is paid in — compute or memory.

Price the compute branch honestly. Re-deriving one past token's K and V means pushing it through two 4,096 × 4,096 projection matrices in each layer: 2 × 2 × 4,096 × 4,096 ≈ 67 MFLOPs, times 32 layers ≈ 2.15 GFLOPs per past token. Step N redoes this for all N predecessors, so a 4,096-token generation pays Σ N ≈ T²/2 re-derivations: about 18 PFLOPs of pure bookkeeping, against 8.8 TFLOPs to compute each token's K,V exactly once — a 2,000× overhead, eighteen seconds of the H100's tensor cores at their full 989 TFLOP/s, spent re-deriving numbers the machine already had. And it lands where it hurts: the final step alone re-derives 8.8 TFLOPs of state to support a forward pass that otherwise costs 14 GFLOPs — 600× more effort remembering than thinking. Recomputation makes the cost of a conversation quadratic in its length, and no hardware roadmap outruns a wrong exponent.

So everyone takes the memory branch, and its price is countable on fingers: 2 tensors (K and V) × 32 layers × 32 KV heads × 128 dimensions = 262,144 numbers, × 2 bytes in fp16 = 524,288 bytes per token. Half a megabyte to remember one word. A 4,096-token conversation therefore carries 2.15 GB of state — a seventh of the model it rides beside — and the 80 GB card holds the 14 GB of weights plus 30 such conversations (78.5 GB) but not 32 (82.8 GB, which does not exist). The trade is exact: quadratic compute exchanged for linear memory, on the one machine where memory capacity and memory bandwidth are precisely the scarce resources.

Look at what kind of state this is, because nothing else on the card behaves like it. The weights are shared, read-only, eternal; one copy serves every user. The KV cache is private to one conversation, append-only, and dies when the request ends — user state, resident in the same HBM as the model. And it is re-read in full on every step: at 30 resident sequences, each decode step streams 64.5 GB of other people's conversations past the ALUs against 14 GB of weights — user state outweighs the model 4.6-to-1 in the read bill. One conversation gets there alone, eventually: at 524 KB per token, a single sequence's cache passes the entire 14 GB model at roughly 26,700 tokens. An inference server is a memory manager for conversations that happens to run matmuls in between; its capacity is measured in resident sessions, not FLOPs.

The industry's response has been to attack the 524 KB one factor at a time. Grouped-query attention (GQA) attacks the heads factor: query heads share K,V in groups, so Llama-3-8B keeps 8 KV heads instead of 32 — 131 KB per token in this geometry, a clean 4×. Multi-query attention (MQA) is the limit case, one KV head serving all 32 query heads: 16 KB per token, 32× smaller, with enough quality cost that the field settled on GQA's middle ground. DeepSeek's multi-head latent attention (MLA) attacks the width instead: cache a low-rank latent vector and reconstruct K,V from it, roughly a tenth the bytes. Mistral's sliding-window attention attacks the length: attend only within a fixed window, and the per-layer cache stops growing past it. Every valve trades a little modeling freedom for bytes, and bytes are concurrency: a 4× smaller cache is 4× more users on the same silicon.

The precision axis multiplies straight through the same product. Quantize the cache alongside the weights and the same card hosts different economies: fp16 holds 30 conversations, int8 (262 KB/token) holds 67, int4 (131 KB/token) holds 142. Notice that int4 full MHA lands exactly where fp16 GQA-8 lands — 131 KB — and the two levers stack in real deployments. The film's model pulls none of them on purpose: full MHA, fp16, 32 KV heads is the worst case, chosen so the pressure stays visible and the arithmetic stays honest. What the valves buy is best read off the crossover: the context length at which one conversation outweighs the whole model moves from about 26,700 tokens at full MHA to about 107,000 at GQA-8.

This is why the title is not hyperbole. Context-window limits are KV budgets denominated in tokens; the batch sizes that set price-per-token are set by how many KV columns fit after the weights; prompt caching — providers billing repeated prefix tokens at a steep discount — is the resale of KV state they already hold in HBM. Even eviction has an exact price: a preempted conversation's cache can be rebuilt by re-prefilling its prefix, and a 2,048-token prefill costs 2.9 × 10¹³ FLOPs — about 29 ms on this H100 — so schedulers weigh bytes held against milliseconds to rebuild. The word cache undersells all of it. The KV cache is not an accelerator bolted onto inference; it is the industry's working set, and a large fraction of what a token costs is the cost of keeping — or rebuilding — this state.

the wrong picture

The KV cache is a performance trick in the memcached sense — an optional layer that speeds up work the model could always redo, safe to evict, share between users, or switch off at the cost of some latency.

what actually happens

It is the mandatory materialization of attention's state. Switch it off and total work goes quadratic — about 18 PFLOPs of K,V re-derivation to generate 4,096 tokens instead of 8.8 TFLOPs once. It cannot be shared across users except where prompts are byte-identical prefixes, and evicting it mid-generation costs a full re-prefill of the prefix to rebuild. It is not a cache sitting in front of the real data; it is the real data.

worth stealing

Any system that must consult its full history at every step faces the same fork: recompute the past, and work grows quadratically, or materialize it, and state grows linearly. Once you materialize, the binding constraint silently moves — capacity is no longer how fast you process but how many sessions' state fit beside the shared code, and the highest-leverage optimization becomes shrinking the per-session record, not buying faster processors. And audit anything named "cache": if losing it changes correctness or costs far more than a refetch, it is primary state wearing a cache's name, and it deserves capacity planning, not eviction heuristics.

05 phase one

Prefill: the tall matmul

The whole trick reduces to bookkeeping. At fp16 a parameter costs 2 bytes to fetch and contributes 2 FLOPs per token it touches — one multiply, one add. One token per fetch is 1 FLOP per byte: that is decode, starving at 0.867 once you charge it for the KV cache too. But the prompt's tokens are all known before the GPU does anything, so they stand up as one N×4096 activation matrix and share every fetch. N tokens per fetch is N FLOPs per byte. The arithmetic intensity of prefill is, to first order, just N. The exact form, with the KV write charged to the denominator, is I(N) = 2PN / (14 GB + N·524 KB) — it crosses the H100's ridge of 295 near N ≈ 300, reaches 502 at N = 512, and as N grows the KV term takes over and intensity saturates near 26,700. That ceiling is not a new number: it is 14 GB over 524 KB, the same ratio that sets the long-context crossover where reading the cache costs as much as reading the model. One ratio, two disguises.

Past the ridge, the max() in the timing model flips sides. For 512 tokens at fp16 the bytes term is 14 GB ÷ 3.35 TB/s ≈ 4.2 ms; the FLOPs term is 2 × 7B × 512 ÷ 989 TFLOP/s = 7.25 ms. The FLOPs term wins, so 7.25 ms is the answer — and now watch what the quantization toggle does. Drop weights to int4 and the bytes term falls to about 1.0 ms; the max() does not move. The same toggle that doubled and quadrupled decode does exactly nothing here. Quantization speeds decode by shrinking bytes; prefill answers only to a faster ALU. The lever that actually moves TTFT is the tensor cores' clock: run the matmuls in FP8 at 1979 TFLOP/s and the same 512-token pass drops to about 3.6 ms. Fewer bits per weight is a bandwidth story; more FLOPs per second is a compute story; prefill lives entirely in the second one.

Why is the tall matmul even legal? Generation is sequential because token t+1's input is token t's output — a true data dependency. Inside a known prompt no such edge exists, and the transformer's structure exploits that almost everywhere for free: roughly two-thirds of the FLOPs are MLP, and the MLP treats each row of the activation matrix independently — 512 tokens through up, gate, down is 512 separate problems that happen to share weights. Attention is the only operator where rows mix, and there causality is enforced by a triangular mask: any score where position i would attend to position j > i gets −∞ added before the softmax, so its weight is exactly zero. Order is preserved by arithmetic, not by time. Nothing waits on anything; every position computes in the same instant; and the K and V tensors the pass banks are the very tensors a strict left-to-right decode would have produced one at a time.

The mask hides a cost. Attention scores form an N×N grid per head, per layer — the one part of prefill that scales quadratically. At the full 4,096-token context that grid is 4096² × 2 bytes ≈ 33.6 MB per head, about 1.07 GB per layer across 32 heads. Materialize that in HBM, then read it back for the softmax and again for the value multiply, and you have handed back the bandwidth the tall matmul just escaped. FlashAttention refuses to write it at all: a block of queries sits in SRAM while blocks of keys and values stream past, and an online softmax — a running max and a running normalizer, with partial outputs rescaled as each tile lands — produces the exact same result while touching HBM only with the N×d inputs and output. The N² FLOPs are unavoidable; the N² bytes never were. And the FLOPs stay affordable: at 2,048 tokens the attention matmuls cost under a tenth of the 2PN weight FLOPs on this 7B model, which is why the timing model can honestly ignore them.

Prefill has a second deliverable, and it is heavier than the first token. As each layer's K and V projections run, the results are kept: 524 KB per token across 32 layers, 268 MB for the 512-token prompt, deposited in HBM as the wall passes through. Writing it is nearly free — 268 MB at 3.35 TB/s is about 80 µs, buried inside the 7.25 ms pass. Reading it back is not free, because decode will re-read the entire deposit on every single step, forever. Prefill's real output is a memory liability that every future decode step must service. Each prompt token is a one-time compute charge of 2P FLOPs and a recurring bandwidth charge of 524 KB per generated token — and past ~26,700 tokens of context the recurring charge outweighs the model itself.

Two latency words now have owners, and they obey different physics. TTFT — time to first token — is queueing plus prefill: compute-bound, moved by faster tensor cores, shorter prompts, or a cached prefix, untouched by weight quantization. TPOT — time per output token — is decode's rhythm: bandwidth-bound, moved by fewer bytes (quantization, GQA) and amortized by batching. This split is printed on every provider's rate card. An input token is one row of an amortized matmul running near a quadrillion FLOPs per second; an output token is a full drag of the working set past the cores. Input tokens are cheap because they arrive in bulk; output tokens are expensive because each one arrives alone. The direction of the price gap — input cheap, output dear — comes straight from the ridge; the magnitude is gentler, because batching amortizes output serving too. Typical rate cards run closer to 3–5× (a $3-in, $15-out pattern) than the raw 295×.

One catch keeps prefill from being purely good news: it is a monolith. A 2,048-token prefill owns the whole GPU for 29 ms, and every co-resident decode stream stalls behind it — at a b8 batch's 9.31 ms step time, roughly three missed token intervals, a visible stutter in everyone else's output. Chunked prefill breaks the monolith: the scheduler gives each iteration a token budget, fills it with pending decode tokens first, then packs in a slice of the waiting tall matmul. The sliced prompt's owner pays slightly more TTFT; every other user's TPOT holds steady. Chunked prefill trades one user's first-token latency for every user's streaming cadence. Follow the logic to its end and the phases separate entirely — prefill/decode disaggregation, where compute-heavy machines run the tall matmuls, bandwidth-heavy machines run the loops, and the KV cache is shipped between them. The two phases were always different workloads; large fleets just stop pretending otherwise.

the wrong picture

The model reads your prompt the way it writes its answer — one token at a time — so a 2,048-token prompt should cost 2,048 decode steps, about 9.9 seconds at 4.82 ms each, and the int4 toggle that doubles decode speed should cut TTFT too.

what actually happens

Prefill consumes every prompt position in one tall matmul: 2,048 tokens cost 29 ms of compute-bound work, roughly 340× less than sequential decode would, because all N rows share each weight fetch. And weight quantization moves TTFT not at all — bytes were never the limit past the ridge. Only more FLOP/s (FP8's 1979 TFLOP/s roughly halves it) or fewer un-cached prompt tokens shortens the wait.

worth stealing

When all the inputs are known upfront, restructure the loop so the expensive shared state streams past once and every input rides the same fetch — batching converts a bandwidth problem into a compute problem. The hardware's compute-to-bandwidth ratio is a fixed exchange rate; the shape of the work, not the silicon, decides which side of it you are on. The same move appears wherever a system has fast state and slow state: columnar scans amortizing one disk read over a million rows, vectorized interpreters amortizing one dispatch over a batch of values. If you are starved on the slow channel, look for work you are doing one at a time that was actually known in advance.

06 phase two

Decode: the memory pump

Prefill ends and the geometry inverts. Generation is autoregressive: token N+1 takes token N as input, and token N does not exist until the previous step has run all 32 layers and the sampler has picked a winner. That is a strict serial dependency — not a scheduling problem, a definitional one. No compiler reorders it, and no second GPU overlaps a token with its successor — tensor parallelism can split each step's weight read across several GPUs' combined bandwidth and shorten the step, but no amount of silicon lets token N+1 begin before token N is sampled. A single stream of text cannot be parallelized with itself. The workload collapses from prefill's thousands-of-rows matrix to the thinnest slice a transformer admits: one row, a 1×4096 activation vector, pushed through every weight matrix in the model to buy one token.

Watch what that does to the arithmetic. Multiplying a matrix by one vector — a GEMV — fetches every weight from HBM, spends it on exactly two floating-point operations, one multiply and one add, and discards it. There is no second row to share the fetch against; the row that could have shared it belongs to a token that has not been sampled yet. So each step reads all 14 GB of fp16 weights, plus the 2.15 GB KV cache at the standing 4096-token context, and performs 2 FLOPs per parameter: 14 GFLOPs against 16.15 GB of traffic. Arithmetic intensity: 0.867 FLOPs per byte, on a machine whose ridge sits at 295. Decode is not slightly memory-bound. It is 340× on the wrong side of the line.

The step time follows from one division. 16.15 GB at 3.35 TB/s is 4.82 ms; the 14 GFLOPs of math, at 989 TFLOP/s, need 14 microseconds and hide entirely inside the transfer. One token every 4.82 ms is 207 tokens per second — the ceiling for one user on one H100, before a single real-world inefficiency is charged. Tensor-core utilization while this happens: 0.29%. That is the figure below — a 4.82-millisecond bar that is almost entirely memory band, with a compute sliver you need a magnifier to find, sweeping again and again, one token per sweep.

This makes batch-1 decode speed a spec-sheet number, and the spec is not FLOPs. Two GPUs of the same memory generation decode a 7B model at nearly the same single-stream rate whatever their tensor-core peaks; the H100's FP8 path doubles compute to 1979 TFLOP/s and changes decode latency not at all, because compute was 0.3% of the budget. The line that matters is bandwidth. Single-stream decode latency is something you buy in bandwidth, not something you engineer in FLOPs — tensor parallelism buys it wholesale, splitting the 14 GB read across several GPUs' aggregate HBM at the price of per-layer all-reduces over NVLink, and when a quantized model decodes twice as fast, it is because the bytes halved (7 GB of int8 weights: 415 tok/s ideal), not because any unit got quicker.

It is worth being precise about why software cannot rescue this, because for GEMM it can. Matrix–matrix kernels earn their speed through reuse: tile the operands into on-chip SRAM and each fetched byte serves hundreds of multiply–adds. GEMV has nothing to reuse — each weight's use count is one multiply–accumulate — and the storage that could hold weights near the cores is hopeless anyway: 50 MB of L2 plus a few hundred kilobytes per SM, against a 14 GB model. The weights outweigh all on-chip memory combined by better than a hundred to one, so every byte crosses the HBM bus on every single step. For decode, a perfect kernel is not one that computes fast; it is one that merely keeps the wire full. Memory-bound at 3.35 TB/s is not a failure mode to optimize away. It is the optimum.

Which is what the real engineering is actually about — and why production stacks still land at a fraction of 207. The ideal charges nothing for getting work onto the GPU. A decode step is hundreds of kernels — on the order of ten per layer, times 32, plus sampling — and each launch costs microseconds of CPU-side dispatch; hundreds of launches at a few microseconds each is over a millisecond, against a 4.82 ms step. CUDA graphs fix this by capturing the step's whole kernel sequence once and replaying it as a single launch; vLLM does this by default, and its --enforce-eager flag exists precisely to turn it off. Fused kernels merge the small memory-bound ops — norms, rotary embeddings, residual adds — so activations stop making extra round trips to HBM between them. Attention has its own hole: at batch 1 the kernel's natural parallelism is batch × heads = 32 thread blocks, on a chip with 132 SMs, so most of the machine sits out the KV read. FlashDecoding splits the cache along the sequence axis, lets every SM stream a chunk, then merges the partial softmaxes in a cheap reduction. None of these tricks shrink the 16.15 GB; they clear the debris around it.

Even with all of it applied, the measured number sits below the roofline: 3.35 TB/s is a peak that sustained access patterns approach but do not touch, sampling and detokenization take their cut, and the scheduler burns time between steps. So treat 207 as what it is — the number the physics permits — and treat serving-stack engineering as the fraction-of-roofline business it is. More important, hold onto the shape of the problem: 16 GB of traffic per token, two FLOPs per weight, a use count of one. Everything that follows attacks that ratio from one of exactly three directions — share the read across users, shrink the read itself, or pull more than one token out of each sweep. Those are the next three chapters, in that order.

the wrong picture

Generating each token is computationally heavy, so single-user chat speed tracks a GPU's TFLOPs — an H100 at 989 TFLOP/s should tear through tokens, and switching on the 2× FP8 tensor cores should roughly double my chatbot's speed.

what actually happens

A batch-1 decode step performs 14 GFLOPs — 14 microseconds of work for the tensor cores — but must stream 16.15 GB of weights and KV to do it. The 4.82 ms step is a memory transfer with a little math inside; speed tracks the 3.35 TB/s bandwidth line on the datasheet, and faster tensor cores change batch-1 latency by nothing. Only more bandwidth (tensor parallelism's aggregate HBM), fewer bytes (quantization), or shared reads (batching) move it.

worth stealing

Before optimizing any loop, compute its arithmetic intensity: work done per byte moved. When the working set dwarfs every cache and each byte is used once, the processor is irrelevant — throughput is the pipe, and a perfect implementation is one that merely saturates it. From there only three moves exist, and they generalize far beyond GPUs: amortize the transfer across more consumers, shrink the bytes, or extract more output per pass. Databases call the first a shared scan, networks call the second compression, and CPUs have called the third speculation for thirty years.

07 the scheduler

Batching, and the server that juggles it

Every decode step pays a fixed toll — the full 14 GB weight read — and the toll belongs to no one in particular. The matrices are identical whether they multiply one hidden state or thirty-two, so stacking B users into one sweep splits the read B ways; only the KV cache is billed per lane. The marginal cost of admitting one more 4k-context user is that user's cache traffic: 2.15 GB per step, 0.64 ms at 3.35 TB/s. Hence the sweep's shape: 207 tok/s alone, 860 at batch 8 (107 per user), 1296 at batch 32 (40 per user). Batching buys total throughput by selling per-user latency — the step stretches from 4.82 ms to 24.7 ms because every user waits on every other user's cache — and by batch 32 the roles have inverted: 68.8 GB of KV against 14 GB of weights, the shared read down to 17 percent of the traffic. The sweep is mostly private cache now.

The hard stop comes before batch 32. Weights plus 32 caches need 82.8 GB; the card has 80. Thirty sequences fit. But capacity is the shallow version of the problem. The deep version: a request's footprint is unknown at admission. KV grows by 524 KB with every token and shrinks only when the sequence dies, and the server cannot know whether a request will stop after twelve tokens or run to the 4096 cap. A serving engine is a memory allocator for objects whose final size is revealed only when they are freed. Reserve every request's worst case and most of the card idles as insurance; reserve the average and the engine will, on some step, need a byte it does not have. Everything that follows — paging, preemption, queues — is machinery for living with that uncertainty.

Static batching — admit B requests, run until all finish — fails on ragged endings. One user asks for a haiku, another for a design document; the haiku's slot sits dead for thousands of steps while the document grinds on, and the amortization the batch existed for decays with each finished lane. Continuous batching, introduced by the Orca serving system, re-forms the batch every iteration: a sequence that emits its stop token this step is gone from the next, and a queued request takes its slot immediately. There is no end of the batch to wait for, because there is no batch — only steps, each with its own membership. What makes the churn free is that the expensive layers never cared about membership. A matmul over eight hidden states is the same kernel whether the eight rows joined together or arrived hours apart; only attention touches per-sequence state, and attention was computed per-sequence already. Orca called this selective batching: batch the layers that batch trivially, split the one that doesn't.

Churn creates the next problem: fragmentation. If each sequence's KV must be contiguous, the allocator must guess a length; pre-vLLM systems reserved the maximum, and a 200-token answer inside a 4096-token reservation wastes 95 percent of its slot. PagedAttention imports the operating-system answer wholesale. The cache is carved into fixed pages of 16 tokens — 8.4 MB each at this model's 524 KB per token — handed out on demand from a free-list; a per-sequence block table maps logical token positions to physical pages, and the attention kernel walks the table. Waste collapses to at most one partially filled tail page per sequence. Pages carry reference counts, so parallel samples from one prompt share their prefix pages copy-on-write instead of duplicating them. The KV cache stopped being a tensor and became virtual memory — same trick, same reason, sixty years later.

So what happens when the free-list runs dry? Nothing errors. Overflow is a scheduling event, not a failure. The engine picks a victim — vLLM evicts a whole sequence, latest-arrived first — and reclaims its pages by one of two routes. Swap: copy the pages to host RAM over PCIe and copy them back when space returns. Recompute: drop the pages outright and, when the sequence is rescheduled, rebuild them by re-running prefill over everything generated so far. Recompute sounds barbaric; arithmetic defends it. Rebuilding 2048 tokens of KV is 29 ms of compute-bound prefill, in the same range as shipping the 1.07 GB across PCIe and back — and it frees the pages instantly instead of tying up the bus. vLLM implements both. Upstream sits admission control: requests that cannot be granted their first pages wait in a queue rather than being admitted on hope. The scheduler's invariant is strict — a running decode step never fails for want of a page — and everything else bends to preserve it.

One interference remains. A new arrival brings a prefill, and prefill is a different animal: 512 tokens run at arithmetic intensity 502, past the ridge of 295, compute-bound. Inject one into a decode batch and every resident stalls behind it — a 7.25 ms prefill nearly doubles the 9.31 ms step of a batch-8 decoder, and every user's cadence hiccups whenever anyone new shows up. Chunked prefill caps the tokens processed per step and slices the prompt across several iterations, riding along with decode instead of displacing it. The mix is better than either phase alone: the prefill chunk barely touches the bandwidth decode is starving on, decode barely touches the compute prefill saturates, and the combined step climbs toward the ridge. The radical version is disaggregation, DistServe-style: separate GPU pools for prefill and decode, the finished cache shipped across the interconnect from one to the other, each pool batched and scaled against its own bottleneck. Interference goes to zero at the price of moving every cache across a wire.

All of this serves two promises. TTFT, time to first token, is the prefill bill plus queueing — what the user perceives as it started. TPOT, time per output token, is the decode cadence — it flows. An operator fixes targets first and then pushes the batch as deep as the targets and the free-list allow, because every knob above trades one promise against the other: deeper batches raise TPOT (9.3 ms per token at batch 8, 24.7 at batch 32 — though on this card memory binds first, at 30 sequences, before a 25 ms TPOT target would at roughly 32; only on a card with more HBM would the latency target become the binding constraint), bigger prefill chunks lower TTFT but jitter TPOT, aggressive admission shortens the queue but invites preemption. The honest scoreboard is goodput — requests completed within their SLOs — not raw tokens per second, which a server can inflate by quietly ruining every individual user's experience. The scheduler's product is not throughput; it is throughput inside a latency contract, and every mechanism in this chapter exists to widen that margin.

the wrong picture

Batch size is a launch-time constant, like in training: the server groups 32 requests, runs them all to completion in lockstep, and if a thirty-third arrives while memory is full, the request fails with an out-of-memory error.

what actually happens

The batch is re-formed at every decode step. Membership churns as sequences finish, arrive, and get preempted; batch size is an emergent number capped by free KV pages, not a configured constant. And running out of memory triggers scheduling — queueing, swapping to host, dropping and recomputing — never a failed request; the engine's invariant is that a running decode step cannot fail for want of a page.

worth stealing

When each cycle of a shared resource carries a large fixed cost, throughput comes from packing more concurrent work into every cycle — until the per-client state you must hold becomes the binding resource instead. At that point the problem changes species: it stops being arithmetic and becomes an operating system. Virtualize the state into fixed pages, allocate from a free-list, preempt and restore under pressure, and admit new work against an explicit latency contract rather than on hope. Kernels did this with virtual memory and databases with buffer pools; GPU serving engines rediscovered the same design because the same constraint reappeared.

08 fewer bytes

Quantization: shrink every byte

Decode is a memory sweep with a clock on it: time equals bytes over bandwidth, and the bandwidth — 3.35 TB/s — is soldered into the package. You cannot negotiate the denominator, so you negotiate the numerator. Every decode step reads all the weights plus this sequence's KV cache: 14 GB + 2.15 GB at 4k context, 4.82 ms, 207 tok/s. Store each weight in eight bits instead of sixteen and the same sweep reads 8.07 GB in 2.41 ms; in four bits, 4.04 GB in 1.21 ms. 207 → 415 → 830 tokens per second, doubling on schedule because the read that dominates the step halves on schedule. The second dividend is capacity: every gigabyte the weights surrender becomes KV room — 30, then 67, then 142 concurrent 4k sequences beside the model — and the batch-32 configuration that wanted 82.8 GB at fp16 runs in 41.2 GB at int8. When latency, throughput, and capacity are all downstream of the same bytes, one encoding change moves all three at once.

What is actually stored is not a small float. A quantized weight is an integer index into a grid — sixteen levels for int4 — plus a scale: one fp16 number shared by a group of 64–128 consecutive weights, mapping indexes back to reals as w ≈ s·q. On the wire, two int4 values pack into each byte; the kernel streams the packed bytes and their scales from HBM, dequantizes into registers, and feeds the tensor cores bf16 exactly as before. The small integers exist only in transit; no integer arithmetic ever happens. These are the weight-only schemes, W8A16 and W4A16: traffic shrinks, FLOPs identical. The dequant instructions are effectively free — at batch 1 the tensor cores sat 0.29% occupied, and the extra register work hides under memory stalls the kernel was already suffering. Do the honest accounting and "int4" costs about 4.16 bits per weight: 4 for the index, 0.125 for an fp16 scale amortized over a group of 128, a hair more for a zero-point.

Round-to-nearest costs at most half a step, so everything depends on the step, and the step depends on the scale: s must reach the largest magnitude in its group. That is the failure mode. One outlier at 4.2 in a group otherwise living inside ±1 stretches s four-fold, and the ordinary weights — the ones carrying most of the information — collapse onto three or four coarse levels. A scale is a promise to represent the largest value it covers, and the whole group pays for that promise. The first defense is scope. Per-tensor quantization lets one outlier tax 45 million weights; per-channel confines the damage to a row; per-group-of-128 confines it to 127 neighbors. Smaller groups buy finer grids at the price of more scale overhead — group size is a dial between fidelity and bytes, and 64–128 is where practice settled.

The second defense is calibration, and it starts by fixing the objective. Round-to-nearest minimizes error in weight space, but nothing downstream reads weights; what propagates is the layer's output. The right target is ‖WX − ŴX‖² over real activations X, and under that objective naive rounding is not optimal. GPTQ exploits the second-order structure: the objective's Hessian is proportional to XXᵀ, computable from a few hundred calibration samples, and GPTQ quantizes a weight matrix one column at a time, folding each column's rounding residual into the not-yet-quantized columns via the inverse Hessian — every error partially compensated by weights still free to move. AWQ reaches the same objective without second-order machinery: measure which input channels the activations actually amplify — roughly 1% are salient — then scale those weight channels up and fold the inverse scale into the preceding operation. The rewrite is mathematically neutral; the rounding error it redistributes is not. Optimize the layer's output, not its weights, and four bits recover most of what naive rounding loses.

FP8 is a different species. Everything above is a storage format — the tensor cores never see the ints. FP8 is an arithmetic format: H100 tensor cores execute it natively at 1979 TFLOP/s, twice the BF16 rate. So FP8 halves the bytes and doubles the peak, and the second effect makes it the first precision here that helps compute-bound prefill (INT8 W8A8, which runs at the same 1979 TOPS, shares this property). The 2048-token prefill costs 2.9 × 10¹³ FLOPs — 29.0 ms against the BF16 peak, roughly 14.7 ms ideal against FP8's. It also moves the map: the ridge sits at peak over bandwidth, and doubling the peak pushes it from 295 to about 591 FLOPs per byte. A higher roof demands twice the arithmetic intensity to reach — which the halved weight reads conveniently supply. The catch: FP8 tensor cores need FP8 on both operands. The weights are easy. The activations are not.

Weight outliers are a frozen problem — the checkpoint sits on disk and you can study it all week. Activation outliers materialize at runtime, per token, and they are systematic: in large transformers a handful of hidden channels run tens to hundreds of times hotter than the rest, the same channels for every input, and a scale wide enough to reach them flattens everything else. That is why W8A8 is the hard tier. SmoothQuant's move is to migrate the difficulty: divide each activation channel by a smoothing factor and multiply the matching weight column by the same factor — an offline, exactly-neutral rewrite that shifts dynamic range from the side that is hard to quantize to the side that is easy — until both fit comfortably in eight bits. At 7B decode scale, weight-only still dominates: decode was never short on math, so cheaper math buys nothing there. Eight-bit activations earn their keep where the tensor cores actually saturate — prefill-heavy and large-batch serving.

The KV cache is the other stream on the wire, and it quantizes too: 524 KB per token at fp16, 262 at eight bits, 131 at four. In production the knob is independent of the weight format, and fp8 KV is the common near-free win — it doubles how many sequences fit and doubles the context length at which KV reads overtake weight reads, from roughly 26,700 tokens to about 53,000 at fp16 weights. What is not free is quality. Each layer's quantization error is small and local by construction; the model is 32 such layers composed, and nothing in the layer-wise objective bounds the end-to-end result. Degradation is graceful until it is not: weight-only int8 is essentially free at this scale, int4 costs a little that good calibration mostly buys back, quantized activations are where the pain concentrates — and the cliff's location depends on model, method, and task in ways no formula predicts. Perplexity can hold flat while a specific capability quietly breaks. The throughput counter measures bytes moved, not answers kept; only evals can tell 830 tok/s of a model from 830 tok/s of its shadow.

the wrong picture

Quantization speeds up inference because integer math is cheaper than floating-point math — the GPU gets to multiply small ints instead of big floats.

what actually happens

In the weight-only schemes that dominate at this scale (W8A16/W4A16), no integer arithmetic happens at all: the ints are a wire format, dequantized in registers, and every multiply still runs in bf16. The speedup is pure memory traffic — reading 4.04 GB per decode step instead of 16.15 — which is why FLOPs-identical int4 still quadruples decode. Only formats the tensor cores execute natively, like FP8 on H100, actually change the speed of the math.

09 idle money

Speculative decoding: spend FLOPs, buy latency

Batching fixed the accountant's problem, not yours. At batch 32 the GPU streams 1296 tokens per second in aggregate — and hands each user 40. (An imaginary operating point, note: fp16 at 4k context, batch 32 needs 82.8 GB of weights plus KV, which does not fit in 80 GB of HBM — treat it as int8 territory or an ideal-machine benchmark.) No batch size changes the fact that your next token cannot exist until a full 14 GB weight read finishes: throughput has a lever, latency does not. Meanwhile the batch-1 machine runs its tensor cores at 0.29 percent utilization — 989 TFLOP/s of silicon doing 2.9 TFLOP/s of work. Speculative decoding treats the idle 99.7 percent as a bankroll: spend arithmetic nobody was using to shorten the one thing batching cannot touch, the serial chain of one-token-after-another.

The purchase works because of a pricing quirk. Generating five tokens costs five sequential weight reads — 24.1 ms. Scoring five tokens that already exist costs one. Hand the target a written-down candidate continuation and the forward pass becomes a five-token prefill: the same 14 GB crosses the bus once, but each weight now multiplies five stacked activations instead of one. FLOPs per sweep rise from 14 GFLOPs to 70; wall-clock stays pinned at 4.82 ms, because the read, not the math, is the clock. Utilization climbs from 0.29 to about 1.5 percent — the bankroll barely notices. Verifying k+1 tokens costs the same sweep as generating one. All that is missing is something cheap to write the candidates.

That something is a 0.35B draft model — 0.7 GB at fp16, and its speed is set by the same physics as the target's: 0.7 GB over 3.35 TB/s is 0.21 ms per token, a miniature weight-read treadmill of its own. It runs k = 4 autoregressive steps, 0.84 ms of guessing, then the target sweeps all five positions — the four guesses plus one beyond — in a single 4.82 ms pass. Cycle time is 5.66 ms, fixed, regardless of outcome; only the payout varies. The target keeps the longest prefix it agrees with. Everything past the first rejection is dead on arrival, because it was conditioned on a token that officially never happened.

"Agrees with" has an exact meaning, and it is the part newcomers underestimate. For each drafted token, accept it with probability min(1, p_target/p_draft); at the first rejection, resample from the leftover distribution (p_target − p_draft)+, normalized. The theorem — Leviathan et al. and Chen et al., 2023 — says the emitted stream is distributed exactly as if the target had written every token. The proof fits in two sentences. The probability a position emits token x via acceptance is p_d(x) · min(1, p_t(x)/p_d(x)) = min(p_d(x), p_t(x)); the total rejection probability is Z = Σ (p_t − p_d)+, and the resample then picks x with probability (p_t(x) − p_d(x))+ / Z — the Z cancels, leaving (p_t(x) − p_d(x))+. Sum the cases: min(p_d, p_t) + (p_t − p_d)+ = p_t(x), exactly, for every x. A bad draft costs you speed, never correctness. And because the verify sweep also computed a distribution one position past the last guess, every cycle banks a bonus token from the target itself — even on total rejection.

Under an independence model — each guess accepted with the same probability a, positions independent, which real text violates but which keeps the arithmetic honest — the expected yield of a cycle is 1 + a + a² + a³ + a⁴: the bonus token plus a geometric series of survivals. At a = 0.9 that is 4.10 tokens per 5.66 ms cycle — 724 tok/s, 3.49× over the 207 baseline. At a = 0.7: 2.77 tokens, 490 tok/s. At a = 0.4: 1.65 tokens, 292 tok/s — a 1.41× that barely earns its keep. The speedup is a property of the text, not the hardware. Code, boilerplate, and quoted passages draft near 0.9; freewheeling prose does not. And independence flatters reality: rejections cluster, because the spans a 0.35B model cannot guess are exactly the spans where the next token is hard too.

Nothing here is free — but be precise about what the bill is. One cycle burns 70 GFLOPs of target verification plus 2.8 GFLOPs of drafting — 72.8 GFLOPs where plain decode spends 14 per token. At a = 0.9 that is 17.8 GFLOPs per emitted token, 1.27× the honest price; at a = 0.4 it is 44, more than triple. FLOPs per token always go up. Energy per token, counterintuitively, tends to go down: the GPU draws roughly the same power either way, and at a = 0.9 it emits ~4.1 tokens per 5.66 ms cycle instead of 1 per 4.82 ms — the dominant energy cost, hauling 14 GB of weights out of HBM, is amortized over four tokens instead of one. The scheme wins at batch 1 because those FLOPs were evaporating anyway — speculation converts compute that was already being wasted into latency — and by this section's own arithmetic the bankroll runs deep: even the (infeasible-at-fp16) batch-32 verify sweep is 448 GFLOPs against an 82.8 GB read, about 5.4 FLOPs per byte versus the 295 ridge — roughly 1.8 percent tensor utilization, still overwhelmingly memory-bound, so verification FLOPs remain nearly free. Speculation only stops paying for itself when batch × (k+1) tokens per sweep approaches the compute ridge — batches in the hundreds, in this ideal model. What actually kills it in real serving stacks at scale is not exhausted compute but KV-cache and slot pressure — every in-flight speculative token holds memory — plus scheduling complexity. Hence the usual placement: a low-batch, latency-tier feature, switched off when the queue runs deep.

The two-model form is the original and the clumsiest — a second network to train, align to the same tokenizer, and keep resident in HBM. The idea has since grown a family, all making the same bet: one expensive sweep can judge many cheap guesses. Medusa bolts extra decoding heads onto the target so it drafts for itself. EAGLE drafts at the feature level: a one-layer head autoregresses over the target's own last hidden states — smoother and more predictable than sampled tokens — then borrows the target's lm_head to turn features into words, and acceptance rises accordingly. DeepSeek's multi-token-prediction heads are the same move made at pretraining time: drafting trained in, not retrofitted. N-gram lookup needs no model at all — it drafts by copying strings already present in the context, which is why it works so well for editing, RAG, and summarization: the output quotes the input. And tree speculation widens the bet — pack a branching tree of continuations into one sequence, craft the attention mask so each node attends only to its own ancestors, and a single forward pass scores every root-to-leaf path at once. The mask does the bookkeeping; the 14 GB is still read once.

the wrong picture

The draft model writes some of your tokens, so speculative decoding must be a speed-for-quality dial — outputs drift toward what a 0.35B model would say, and you should only enable it when slightly dumber text is acceptable.

what actually happens

The draft proposes; it never decides. The accept rule min(1, p_target/p_draft) plus the (p_target − p_draft)+ resample makes the emitted stream provably identical to the target's own distribution, token for token — the two cases sum back to p_target exactly. A weaker draft lowers acceptance and therefore speed; quality is mathematically untouched. The real bill lands on FLOPs per token, which go up, always — though since the GPU's power draw barely moves and more tokens leave per weight read, energy per token typically goes down.

worth stealing

When a system is latency-bound on a serial dependency chain while its parallel capacity sits idle, convert the chain into guess-and-verify: a cheap predictor runs ahead, an expensive authority validates many guesses in one batched pass, and mis-speculations roll back exactly. CPUs do it with branch prediction, databases with optimistic concurrency control, compilers with prefetching. The price is wasted work, so the trade is only rational while capacity would otherwise evaporate — apply the same trick to a saturated system and it subtracts throughput instead of adding speed. Price speculation against current utilization, never against zero.

10 the growing state

The long-context regime

Every number in this piece so far has quietly pinned the context at 4,096 tokens. Un-pin it. The decode model says a batch-1 step must read 14 GB + ctx × 524 KB from HBM, and only the first term is constant. Each token the model processes appends 524 KB of K and V that every subsequent step must re-read — at 3.35 TB/s, about 0.16 microseconds added to every future step, forever, for the life of the sequence. At fixed batch, TPOT does not degrade with load or with bad luck; it degrades linearly with the length of the conversation itself. A generation slows down as it runs. Token 5,000 of an answer is strictly more expensive than token 1, and nothing recovers the difference except ending the sequence.

There is a crossover, and it is computable. KV read equals weight read when ctx × 524 KB = 14 GB — about 26,700 tokens for this model at fp16 with full multi-head attention. At that length a decode step moves 28 GB instead of 16.15, and TPOT goes from 4.82 ms to 8.36 ms: the same GPU, the same model, 120 tokens per second instead of 207. Past 26,700 tokens, the thing you are pumping through HBM every step is no longer mostly the model — it is the conversation. The weights become the fixed overhead; the cache becomes the workload. Everything the roofline said about weight-bound decode still holds, except the word "weight" now means "state."

Push to 128k and the model stops being merely slow and becomes impossible. One sequence at 128,000 tokens of fp16 MHA cache is 67 GB — nearly five full copies of the weights — and 67 + 14 = 81 GB does not fit in 80 GB of HBM. One user. Zero headroom. And capacity dies before you get there: at 4k, 30 sequences share the card; at the crossover, each sequence's cache is a second copy of the model and only 4 fit. Long context does not just tax latency — it destroys the batching that made serving economical in the first place, because every sequence's private state crowds out the neighbors that were supposed to share the weight read.

This arithmetic — not benchmark scores — is why frontier models ship grouped-query attention. GQA-8 keeps all 32 query heads but shares 8 K/V heads among them: the cache shrinks 4×, to 131 KB per token, and the crossover retreats from 26,700 to about 107,000 tokens. A 128k sequence drops from 67 GB to 16.8 GB and fits with room for batchmates. DeepSeek's MLA goes further, projecting K and V into a low-rank latent per token and reconstructing heads on the fly, cutting cache well below GQA. Attention architecture is now designed backward from the serving bill: the question is not "what can the heads express" but "how few bytes must each token leave behind."

Decode is linear in context, but prefill is not — and at 100k+ the quadratic finally bites. Attention's score-and-blend work is roughly 4 × N² × d_model FLOPs per layer, about 524,288 × N² across all 32. At a 4k prefill that is 8.8e12 FLOPs, a 15% rider on the 5.7e13 the weight matmuls cost — ignorable, which is why the model ignored it. At 128k it is 8.6e15 against 1.8e15: attention is now five times the matmul bill. Worse, the naive implementation materializes an N×N score matrix — 32.8 GB per head per layer at 128k in fp16, about a thousand times over (32 heads × 32 layers = 1,024). FlashAttention does not reduce the quadratic work; it tiles the computation through on-chip SRAM so the quadratic work never becomes quadratic HBM traffic — keeping long prefill compute-bound, which is the side of the roofline the GPU can actually win.

The economics shift with the bytes. Weights are read once per step no matter how many sequences ride the batch — they amortize. The cache is private: 524 KB per token per user, read every step, shareable with no one. An agent that carries a 60k-token trace, or a RAG pipeline that stuffs 40k tokens of retrieved documents into every request, is past the crossover on every single call — paying more per step to re-read its own history than to run the model. Weights amortize across the batch; the cache never does — which is why long-context requests cost providers disproportionately more, and why the pricing pages say so.

There is one economic escape hatch, and it previews the next section: most long contexts are not unique. The system prompt, the retrieved documents, every prior turn of the chat — the same prefix, prefilled again on every request. Re-prefilling 2,048 tokens costs 2.9e13 FLOPs and 29 ms; at 100k the quadratic pushes a single prefill to roughly 6.6e15 FLOPs — about seven seconds of an H100's entire dense-BF16 throughput at perfect utilization, repeated per turn. Prefix caching keeps the prefix's KV resident and skips the recompute entirely: the request pays a cache read instead of a prefill. Prefix caching converts the most expensive operation in serving — recomputing attention over an unchanged prefix — into the cheapest one, a memory lookup, and it is why providers price cached input tokens several times below fresh ones.

the wrong picture

Long context makes generation slow because attention is O(N²) — each new token costs quadratically more, so decode grinds to a halt at 100k.

what actually happens

Decode is linear: each step reads the cache exactly once, so bytes = 14 GB + ctx × 524 KB and TPOT grows a fixed 0.16 µs per token of context. The quadratic lives only in prefill. What actually breaks decode at long context is memory — 524 KB/token of HBM that crowds out batchmates until, at 128k fp16 MHA, the 67 GB cache plus the 14 GB of weights overflows the 80 GB card. That is why the real fixes are byte-shrinkers (GQA, MLA, KV quantization), not faster arithmetic.

11 more machines

Beyond one GPU

Everything so far ran on one H100, and one H100 runs out in two different ways. It runs out of room: a 70B model at fp16 weighs 140 GB of weights alone, a 405B model still weighs 405 GB even squeezed to fp8, and the card has 80 GB. And it runs out of speed: decode at batch 1 is a memory sweep, the sweep runs at exactly 3.35 TB/s, and no software cleverness changes that number. You go multi-GPU for two different reasons — capacity and bandwidth — and the sharding scheme you pick decides which one you actually get.

Tensor parallelism shards every matrix. Cut each weight matrix into G slices — attention splits cleanly along heads (32 heads across 4 GPUs is 8 heads each, and the KV cache shards with them), the MLP splits along its 11008 hidden columns. Each GPU now holds 1/G of every layer, and each decode step sweeps 1/G of the bytes. For the 7B at G=4: 3.5 GB of weights per GPU instead of 14, and the weight sweep drops from 4.18 ms to about 1.05 ms — four memory systems, 13.4 TB/s aggregate, sweeping in lockstep. Tensor parallelism is the rare knob that shortens per-token latency itself; batching raises throughput but slows every user's clock, TP speeds the clock. The same arithmetic is what makes a 70B servable at all: 140 GB across 8 GPUs is 17.5 GB per GPU per token, a 5.2 ms sweep — the same league as the unsharded 7B.

The price is a conversation per layer. A sharded matmul produces partial sums: after the attention output projection and again after the MLP down-projection, each GPU holds an incomplete d_model-wide vector and must add it to everyone else's — an all-reduce, roughly 2 × d_model × batch × 2 bytes of activations, about 16 KB per layer at batch 1. The bytes are trivial. The synchronization is not: two all-reduces per layer times 32 layers is 64 forced rendezvous per token, each a round trip over NVLink, each costing microseconds of latency regardless of payload. NVLink4 moves ~900 GB/s aggregate per GPU, so at batch 1 this tax is a few percent; but the comms bill is fixed per layer while the per-GPU sweep shrinks as 1/G, so doubling G keeps halving the useful work and never touches the overhead. Past the 8-GPU NVLink island of a single node, the links drop to network speeds and the arithmetic collapses entirely. That is why tensor parallelism almost never crosses a node boundary.

Pipeline parallelism makes the other cut: layers into stages. GPU 0 takes layers 1–16, GPU 1 takes 17–32; the only traffic between them is one activation vector per token per boundary — 4096 values, 8 KB at fp16 — instead of an all-reduce per layer. This is capacity almost for free: no NVLink budget, tolerant of ordinary networks, stack as many stages as the model needs. But look at what it does not buy. Each token still visits every layer in order, each stage sweeps its own weights at its own single-GPU 3.35 TB/s, and the per-token latency is unchanged. Worse, with one request in flight, G−1 stages sit idle — the pipeline bubble. Prefill fills the bubbles with microbatches; decode fills them with other users' tokens, which makes pipeline parallelism a batching scheme wearing a topology. Pipeline parallelism buys capacity and throughput, never latency.

Mixture-of-experts attacks the bill itself. Decode's cost is bytes per token; a dense model makes every token pay for every parameter. MoE replaces each MLP with N expert MLPs and a small router, and each token activates only k of them — top-2 of 8, say. Now total parameters set what the model knows, while active parameters set what each token reads: 8 experts of capacity, 2 experts of bytes. MoE decouples the two currencies of decode — capacity and bytes per token — which is why frontier models are MoE. Expert parallelism shards the experts across GPUs, and routing becomes an all-to-all: each token's 8 KB activation ships to the GPUs hosting its chosen experts, gets processed, ships back. The failure mode is herding — if the router sends every token to the same expert, one GPU sweeps while seven idle, which is why routers are trained with load-balancing losses and served with capacity limits.

Zoom out once more and the fleet itself gets sharded — by phase. Prefill at 512 tokens runs at arithmetic intensity 502, far above the H100's ridge of 295: compute-bound, tensor cores saturated. Decode at batch 1 runs at 0.867: bandwidth-bound, tensor cores dark. These are opposite machines wearing the same silicon, and colocating them means a 29 ms prefill for one user's 2048-token prompt stalls every other user's 5 ms decode steps. So modern stacks disaggregate: a prefill pool sized by incoming prompt tokens, a decode pool sized by generated tokens, scaled independently as the workload's input/output ratio shifts. The handoff is the KV cache — 2.15 GB for a 4k-token sequence at fp16 — shipped from prefill GPU to decode GPU: about 2.4 ms over NVLink-class links, an order of magnitude more over a datacenter network. Disaggregation lets you buy compute and bandwidth separately — and it makes every KV-shrinking trick pay twice, once in HBM and once in transit.

The pattern recurses. Inside the chip: 132 SMs on one side, 80 GB of HBM on the other, a 3.35 TB/s wire, ridge at 295. Inside the node: 8 GPUs and an NVLink fabric roughly 4× slower than HBM. Across the datacenter: pools of nodes and a network an order of magnitude slower still. At every level there is a fast local resource, a slower interconnect, and a scheme for deciding what moves — all-reduce activations, forward activations, routed tokens, shipped KV. The fleet is the chip, one level up: the same roofline drawn at coarser scale, and every technique in this section is one more way of trading bytes moved against work done.

the wrong picture

Parallelism is parallelism: shard a model across 8 GPUs and each token comes out 8× faster, the same way 8 workers finish a job 8× sooner.

what actually happens

Only tensor parallelism shortens the per-token sweep — and only until the fixed per-layer all-reduce tax catches the shrinking 1/G sweep, which is why it stops at the node's NVLink boundary. Pipeline parallelism leaves per-token latency exactly where it was: every token still walks all 32 layers in sequence; you bought room and throughput, not speed. Which resource you shard — bytes per step, or layers per box — decides which metric moves.

worth stealing

When one machine can't carry a workload there are only two cuts: split the state, so every worker holds a slice and you aggregate bandwidth but pay a synchronization tax on every step; or split the pipeline, so every worker owns a stage and you aggregate capacity but pay in idle bubbles. 12 the invoice

The price of a token

Token pricing is one division: dollars per token equals the price of a GPU-hour divided by the tokens that hour produces. The numerator is boring — an H100 rents for roughly the same whether it idles or saturates; call it $2 an hour, an illustrative figure, not a quote. The denominator is everything this film built. At batch 1, fp16, the 7B model decodes 207 tokens per second: 745,000 tokens an hour, $2.68 per million tokens. At batch 32, int8, the same GPU emits 2,592 per second: 9.3 million an hour, $0.21 per million. That is a 12× price spread on identical silicon, and the entire pricing sheet of every inference provider is downstream of this one division.

Nothing physical changed between those two numbers. Same chip, same clocks, same rent. What changed is the operating point: batch 1 at fp16 uses 0.29 percent of the machine's compute, and batching plus quantization climbs toward the ridge. Push further — int4 at batch 32 gives 5,184 tokens per second, 18.7 million an hour, $0.11 per million — and the spread stretches to 25×, bought with quantization error you must measure rather than assume away. Price per token is not a property of the model; it is a property of the operating point the provider runs it at. When two providers quote different prices for the same open-weights model, you are mostly reading their batch sizes, their precisions, and their willingness to make you wait.

Every pricing page charges more for output tokens than input tokens, and the reason is not that generating is harder than reading. The FLOPs per token are identical in both directions — about 2 per parameter, 14 GFLOPs for the 7B model, whether the token is consumed or produced. The difference is machine time. Prefill processes the prompt as one tall matmul: 2,048 tokens in 29.0 milliseconds is about 71,000 input tokens per second from a single sequence — under a cent per million tokens of raw compute at the illustrative rental. Decode at batch 1 manages 207 per second, because each output token buys a share of a full 14 GB weight sweep while each input token splits one compute-bound pass with two thousand neighbors. Output prices above input because decode pays for memory sweeps and prefill amortizes them. The published ratio is usually 3 to 5×, not 340×, because production decode runs batched and because input tokens carry a cost forward: every prompt token adds 524 KB of KV that every later decode step must read.

Batch APIs serve the same model at roughly half the list price, and the discount is the provider choosing the corner for you. A batch job waives its latency claim: no time-to-first-token target, no per-user tokens-per-second floor, a completion window measured in hours. Freed from the SLO, the scheduler packs the work at large batch, runs it in the off-peak troughs of the interactive traffic curve, and preempts it whenever chat load spikes. Machine time per token at the throughput corner sits severalfold below the chat corner, so half price still improves the provider's margin. The batch discount is not generosity; it is the market price of your patience, and patience is precisely what moves work from the expensive corner of the roofline to the cheap one.

Prefix caching monetizes a mathematical fact: the KV cache for a prefix is a pure function of the prefix tokens. Two requests that share a system prompt share, bit for bit, the keys and values at those positions — so the provider prefills the shared prefix once, pins its KV, and every subsequent request skips that slice of prefill compute entirely. What remains is rent, not work: a 2,048-token cached prefix at fp16 occupies 1.07 GB of HBM, held against the chance you return. That is why cached input tokens price severalfold below uncached ones — the matmuls are simply gone from the bill. The KV cache is a memoization table, and cached-input pricing is memoization passed through to the invoice. Tiering extends it: hot prefixes stay in HBM, warm ones spill to host DRAM and stream back over PCIe faster than recomputation, and a long-lived agent's conversation history becomes a cache entry instead of a repeated prefill.

The same fleet therefore sells two products from opposite corners of one chart. The chat product lives at the latency corner: small batches, quantized weights to shrink the sweep, speculative decoding to multiply single-stream speed — 724 tokens per second at batch 1 with a 0.9-acceptance draft, against 207 without. Its tokens are expensive because few users share each pass over the model. The batch product lives at the throughput corner: batch as high as HBM allows, no draft model — speculation spends spare compute, and near the ridge there is none spare — and every millisecond of the sweep split across dozens of sequences. A pricing page is a roofline diagram with dollar signs: each line item names the corner where that product runs. The fleet scheduler's whole job is to interleave the two so no weight sweep ever runs underfilled.

Read a real pricing page with this arithmetic in hand and the line items stop being marketing. Output above input: sweeps versus amortization. Cached input far below uncached: skipped matmuls, retained storage. Batch tier at half: latency surrendered, corner changed. Prices falling year over year at constant quality: quantization, better kernels, and new silicon lifting both the roof and the ridge. None of it requires insider knowledge — every line follows from a weight count, a bandwidth figure, and a rental rate, and you can now recompute the whole invoice from first principles.

the wrong picture

Output tokens cost more than input tokens because generating text is computationally harder than reading it — the model must 'work harder' to write a token than to ingest one.

what actually happens

The FLOPs are identical either way: about 2 per parameter per token, read or written. The gap is machine time. Prefill amortizes one weight pass across thousands of prompt tokens at compute-bound rates (~71,000 tok/s for the 7B), while decode pays a full 14 GB weight sweep per step, shared only across the batch (207 tok/s at batch 1). Output tokens buy sweeps; input tokens split one.

Prefix blocks evicted under memory pressure are freed and recomputed on the next miss — spilling prefix KV to host DRAM takes a separate offloading connector such as LMCache.
worth stealing

When fixed costs dominate and marginal cost is near zero, the price of a unit of work is set by utilization, not by the work itself — the same machine spans an order of magnitude in unit cost depending on how full you run it. The profitable response is to segment customers by latency tolerance and sell the idle capacity cheap: airlines do it with seats, grids do it with off-peak power, GPU fleets do it with batch tiers. When a pricing page confuses you, ask what operating point each line item implies rather than what the work intrinsically costs.

13 what to steal

Patterns worth stealing

Close the film the way an engineer would: with the division that opened it. Before profiling anything, compute two numbers — the machine's exchange rate (peak work per second over peak bytes per second) and the workload's intensity (work done per byte touched). For the H100 that was 989 ÷ 3.35 ≈ 295 FLOPs per byte; for single-stream decode it was 0.867. The bottleneck was decidable on paper, and it usually is. Every system has a ridge — requests per disk seek, messages per round trip, rows per cache line — and an afternoon of arithmetic against it is worth a week of profiling without it.

Once the arithmetic says memory-bound, only one currency is left. Decode's floor is 16.2 GB ÷ 3.35 TB/s = 4.82 ms, and no term in that expression counts a FLOP: halve the compute and nothing moves; halve the bytes and everything doubles. On the wrong side of the ridge, optimize what you move, not what you do. The same discipline governs any I/O-bound system — columnar layouts that read only the columns queried, compression on the wire, delta sync instead of full sync. All are one move: shrink the bytes, because bytes are the denominator.

The most expensive read in the system — the full 14 GB weight scan — happens every step no matter what. Batching does not make it cheaper; it makes it shared. Eight sequences riding one scan turned 207 tokens per second into 860, at the price of each user waiting 9.31 ms instead of 4.82. When a fixed cost dominates, recruit more consumers for it rather than trying to shrink it. Databases have run shared scans across concurrent queries for decades; any per-request cost that does not scale with request count is an amortization opportunity wearing a latency tax.

Single-stream decode leaves 99.7% of the tensor cores idle, and speculation spends them: a small draft guesses four tokens, the target verifies all five positions in one pass, and at 90% agreement the stream runs 3.49× faster — 724 tokens per second bought with compute that was doing nothing. An idle resource is not waste to lament; it is a budget to spend on whatever you lack. CPUs spend idle execution units on speculative execution down predicted branches; prefetchers spend spare bandwidth to hide latency. The recipe is constant: guess with the abundant resource, verify cheaply, and arrange for wrong guesses to cost only what was idle anyway.

The KV cache began as memoization — 524 KB per token that would cost 2.9 × 10¹³ FLOPs to recompute for a 2048-token prompt — and ended as the thing being managed: past roughly 26,700 tokens of context, reading the cache outweighs reading the model itself. Derived state you cannot afford to recompute stops being an optimization and becomes a resource, and resources need an operating system. Hence PagedAttention's block tables, which are page tables; prefix caching, which is shared pages with copy-on-write; eviction policy, which is swap. If your service memoizes anything large — materialized views, JIT code caches, rendered thumbnails — design the allocator, the eviction, and the sharing story on purpose, because they will exist either way.

Every millisecond in this film came from one line — t = max(bytes ÷ bandwidth, FLOPs ÷ peak) — and none came from a profiler. That is the method, not a shortcut. A ceiling derived from first principles is what makes a measurement mean something. Measure 150 tokens per second against an ideal of 207 and you know you are at 72% of light speed and roughly where the loss lives; measure 150 in a vacuum and you know a number. The ideal model is deliberately wrong — it ignored attention FLOPs and assumed perfect overlap, and said so — but a disclosed simplification you can reason under beats an accurate simulation you cannot. Build the napkin model before the benchmark, whatever the system: queueing delay, fan-out latency, replication lag.

Prefill and decode share weights, a chip, and nothing else: 502 FLOPs per byte against 0.867, a compute-bound phase and a memory-bound one taking turns on silicon that can only be right for one of them. Disaggregated serving splits them onto separate pools — prefill machines bought for FLOPs, decode machines bought for bandwidth and HBM capacity — and pays a 2.15 GB KV transfer for the privilege. When one pipeline has two bottlenecks, it wants two machines. The seam is everywhere once you look: ingest versus query, encode versus serve, build versus run. Split where the bound changes, and size each side for what actually limits it.

Precision is the one knob that moves every denominator at once: dropping fp16 to int4 shrank the weights from 14 GB to 3.5, quadrupled the decode ceiling from 207 to 830 tokens per second, and raised the resident-sequence budget from 30 to 142. But it is a budget, not a dial — spent where the consumer cannot tell the difference. Weights tolerate 4 bits; the KV cache survives 8; accumulations stay in higher precision; the logits stay clean. Most systems ship more resolution than any consumer ever reads, and the surplus is bytes you are paying to move. Audit what each reader of your data actually requires — timestamps, coordinates, feature-store floats — and reclaim the rest.

the wrong picture

Inference optimization is a grab-bag of GPU-era tricks discovered empirically: you profile, find hotspots, and apply whichever vendor hack fits. Batching, paged caches, and speculation are LLM-specific inventions that will not transfer to your systems.

what actually happens

Nearly every technique in this film was derivable from one division — 989 TFLOP/s ÷ 3.35 TB/s = 295 — before any profiler ran, and each is an old systems pattern in new clothes: batching is the database shared scan, PagedAttention is virtual memory, prefix caching is copy-on-write, speculative decoding is branch prediction with an exact verifier. Learn the ratio and the toolbox assembles itself.

worth stealing

Every system has an exchange rate: divide its peak work rate by its peak movement rate, then compare that against the workload's own ratio of work to movement. That single division tells you which currency — cycles or bytes — your optimizations must be denominated in, and effort spent in the wrong currency buys nothing. Once the scarce currency is known, the moves are generic: amortize the expensive access across consumers, spend the idle resource to purchase the scarce one, and manage any state too expensive to recompute the way an OS manages memory.
